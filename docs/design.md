# Game design

Status on 2026-11-12: the design below is agreed and the foundation is being built. The game has no name yet.

This document records what we decided, why, and what we set aside. Change it when a decision changes.

## The game in one paragraph

A single-player grand strategy game that runs in the browser. You rule one of five classical powers on a real map that reaches from Iberia to the Iranian plateau. You grow cities, raise armies, trade, make treaties, and take land. Everything happens in real time on the one map, battles included, so the difficulty comes from watching several fronts at once. You march armies to meet a threat, and you move people out of a city before it falls.

The game is original. Civilization 5, Knights of Honor 2, and Rome: Total War 2 are reference points for mechanics and period. The game copies no names, art, or maps from them.

## Game design decisions

### Setting

- **Map extent.** The Mediterranean world, from Iberia to Persia and from Britain to the Nile, extended east across the Iranian plateau. Persia has a full homeland and does not sit on the map edge. We set aside a whole Old World map and a full globe because they multiply content and dilute the period.
- **Era.** A loosely historical sandbox. The map, cities, and cultures are real, but there is no exact start date. Each great power starts as a comparable regional state in its homeland. We set aside the historical starts of 500 BC, 280 BC, and 60 BC because each one makes Persia either a subject people, a lone superpower, or one half of a two-sided contest.
- **Great powers.** Rome, Carthage, Macedon, Egypt, and Persia. Independent minor cities fill the land between them. Persia is a first-class faction and is playable from the first build.
- **Faction differences.** All factions share the same buildings, economy, and basic troop types. Each great power adds a signature of two or three unique units, one unique building, and one standing bonus. Persia, for example, could field cataphracts and horse archers and build royal roads that speed movement.

### Map and time

- **Provinces with free-moving armies.** The map is divided into provinces, and each province is anchored by one city. When the city changes owner, the province changes owner. Armies are markers that march continuously across the terrain. You can see a threat approach, intercept it, or leave ahead of it. We set aside a hex grid and province-to-province movement because both make interception coarse.
- **Clock.** The clock runs continuously with speed settings. You can pause at any time and give orders while paused.
- **Fog of war.** You see enemy markers only within sight range of your land, your armies, and your allies. The evacuation decision depends on how early you see an army.

### War

- **Armies are lists of formations.** A formation is one body of one troop type, such as spearmen or light cavalry. Battles resolve formation by formation.
- **Battles.** When hostile armies meet, they lock together on the map and the battle runs over game days. Losses and morale depend on numbers, troop types, terrain, the general, and fortifications. You steer a battle with an army-wide stance, with reinforcements, and with retreat. The stances are hold the line, all-out assault, and skirmish and withdraw.
- **Room for direct control later.** A stance is one order applied to all formations in an army. Direct command of single formations on the map can come later by exposing the formations, without new battle rules.
- **Sieges.** A walled city must be besieged. A siege runs over game days and wears down walls, garrison, and food. A relief army can break the siege from outside. The attacker can storm the walls early at a cost in men.
- **Fate of a captured city.** The winner chooses to occupy the city, to sack it for gold at the cost of population and buildings, or to enslave part of the population and take them home.
- **Tuning target.** Conquest must feel rewarding without being trivial. A siege lasts weeks of game time, not years. Storming is a sound choice for a clearly stronger army. An occupied city pays off soon after it falls. Playtests set the numbers.
- **Tuning numbers.** Set after the first playtest and checked with `npm run sim:ai` on 20 seeds. A minor city holds a garrison of 1,200 behind walls of 100, a great power's city 1,500 behind 200, and a capital 3,000 behind 400. A siege starves a city only with at least as many men as its garrison, and a weaker siege suffers sallies. A storm must beat the walled garrison one and a half times over, so a clearly stronger army takes intact walls for a fifth to a third of its men, and a weaker one fails. A conqueror leaves a quarter of the full garrison in the city from its own men. A city that falls holds at least half its full food, the harvest its siege kept outside, and a tenth of its full walls, the breach its conquerors close, so a city taken by hunger is not starved or given up by the next siege within days, and a great-power city holds a besieger who only matches its garrison for more than a week. Recruits in a besieged city muster at once to defend it. A sack pays six months of the city's gold, so an occupied city out-earns a sacked one within about eight months, and a city the conqueror will lose soon is still better sacked, kills two fifths of its people, and halves its gold for three years. Enslaving carries off three tenths of the people, a quarter of them as manpower. Over five years under the AI, about two thirds of the minor cities remain at the end of year two and about half at the end of year five, the powers fight each other from year two, and a power that stays at home is attacked by a neighbour twice as strong.
- **Battle tuning.** Agreed after the first full playtest, when battles were over in a day and beaten armies died out of sight. Two armies of about the same strength fight 6 to 12 game days, a side outnumbered two to one holds at least 3, and only a remnant of about 50 men can be wiped out on the first day. Numbers and troop types decide most battles. `npm run sim:battles` prints the table these targets are checked against.
- **Stances answer each other.** Agreed after the second playtest, when every stance lost to the all-out assault and none decided a battle. Each stance beats one other: hold the line beats an all-out assault, an assault beats skirmish and withdraw, and skirmishers beat a line that holds. The side whose stance beats the enemy's strikes harder and keeps its nerve longer, so the right stance is worth about a quarter more men. In an even battle the better stance wins nearly always, and a side outnumbered 1.3 to 1 wins about half its battles with the stance that answers the enemy's. An assault is bloodier and a skirmish lighter on both sides, except that two armies that both skirmish cannot both keep their distance and strike at normal blows. A change of stance is a commitment: the army re-forms for three days, and every enemy that is not re-forming meets it as the stance that beats it, so a change a day is always beaten and a player answers an enemy once, when it pays. The AI answers its enemy's stance by the same rule: it chooses freely once in a battle, and after that changes only when the enemy's stance beats its own and neither side is re-forming.
- **A world that stays open.** Agreed after the second playtest, when one power took every city by year 28. A realm of more than eight cities governs each less well: each yields a falling share of its gold and manpower, so income grows ever more slowly with size. A great power that holds a fifth of all cities, and more than any other, is the leader, and its neighbours declare war on it at odds they would not take against another rival. A power that has grown needs better odds to start a war on a minor city, so peace between the powers does not turn every freed army on the minors. A power past eight cities needs worse odds to start a war on a rival great power whose treasury is full, by more the further it has grown, three eighths less at sixteen cities, since the gold is what a peace makes the rival pay: agreed after the sixth playtest, when a cautious Rome of four cities and 5,800 men was attacked by no great power in ten years, because a realm of twenty cities, past the cost of governing, fields no more men than Rome. The news tells the player when a great power at peace with it comes within a fifth of the strength it needs, and how much the treasury takes off. A great power declares war on the player only after it has stood within that fifth for 30 days, and the news warns of each such stand once it has lasted a week, and again 60 days after each day it named while the stand lasts, so a war on the player always follows a warning of the stand it declares on by 24 days or more and never comes long after the day it was told of: agreed after the ninth playtest, when Macedon declared five years after its only warning and other powers on the day after theirs, and after the tenth, when a stand that came back within the year was warned of only 11 days before its war, and a war came 152 days after the day its warning named. A warning stops the game, but a power that stopped it does not stop it again for a while: a later warning of a new notice of the same power runs the game on, told in the corner, the At peace list and the chronicle with the day and share of the stop it repeats, while any war it could bring would still come within 200 days of that stop, unless it weighs another of the player's cities or its field armies, or stands 6 points or more higher; a reminder still stops it: agreed after the thirteenth play-through, when Carthage stopped the game at Ariminum on Year 5, days 211, 248, 278 and 311 at 80 to 85% each time, and the player had nothing new to decide at the last three. A power on notice that stands 6 points or more above the share the game last stopped for stops it again, and guards it counted that stop counting are told on that day without a stop: agreed when a measured Rome that answered every warning met Carthage's war unstopped, the day after Carthage climbed from the 82% it had last stopped the game at to 100%, and the owner accepted the 1.25 more stops a game it costs a Rome that ignores warnings. A warning of a power that a war holds back names that war and the share the power would stand at once it ends, and the day such a war ends is told without a stop: agreed when Carthage, held at 82 to 86% by its war with Londinium, stood at 100% the day Londinium fell, and no warning had named the war. A warning whose advice would have the player raise men the realm's food cannot feed says that they would desert, and names what would feed them, or that guards eat nothing: agreed when a measured Rome that answered every warning raised 1,900 men in 85 days and lost 1,849 to desertion before the war came. A siege too weak to cut the food and with no help on the way is left before the sallies destroy it, and with no way home its army steps out onto the land nearby. Checked with `npm run sim:world` on seeds 1 to 20 and 21 to 40: at year 10 the largest power holds at most half the cities and at least three powers live in nearly every seed.
- **Minor cities differ.** Each minor is drawn from the seed as one of four kinds: a standing army of about 1,200 men with stores for two years, a rich and walled city with more people, half again the walls and food, and gold to raise troops, a poor and open city with few people, half the walls, and little food, and a city leagued with its nearest leagued neighbour, which joins every war on its partner and marches to lift its siege. A minor far from every great power's capital has guarded itself and is more often one of the first two. So no two first conquests are the same, and a battle against a standing army lasts days.
- **Retreat and pursuit.** A beaten or retreating army breaks off and falls back to the nearest city of its own that is not besieged and that no enemy threatens. With none, it falls back into the field, away from the enemy. The winner's horsemen cut down part of it as it breaks, and it fights no battle until it arrives. An enemy that marches after it can fight it again once it stands still. The winner takes up the march it was on, so an order survives a battle on the way.
- **News.** Every event that touches the player, or that happens in sight, reaches the alerts: a battle that begins, a battle that ends with its result and losses, a retreat, a lost army, desertion, and a declaration of war on the player. This holds for an event that begins and ends within one day.
- **Generals.** A general is a skill number on an army for now, from 0 to 10. The best general against none does not make up for a fifth more men. Every new army, at the start or raised by recruits, has a general of skill 3.
- **The sea.** An army embarks at a port city and crosses the water as a transport convoy. A convoy is slow and fights badly. War fleets come in a later stage. Fleets fight sea battles, capture convoys, blockade ports, and protect sea trade.

### Country

- **Population.** Each city has a population number. Population drives taxes, food use, production, and recruits. A city grows 4 % a year while its owner's food lasts and shrinks 10 % a year while it is gone. Agreed after the eleventh play-through, whose testers called everything after year 5 a slow slide. At 2 % a peaceful Rome met its call-up limit in year 3, and its army could then grow by only about 90 men a year while its rivals grew by conquest. One raise of 500 Skirmishers also put the next raise off by nearly seven years. At 4 % the call-up limit, the food the land grows, the granaries and the treasury all grow twice as fast. Over seeds 1 to 20 for ten years (`npm run sim -- slide`), a Rome that ignores warnings lost 13 cities in all instead of 89, and one that answers them lost 15 instead of 40. In the thirteenth play-through the Rome that answered every warning still lost three of its four cities, and over those seeds the one that answered lost 15 cities against 13 for one that ignored them. `npm run sim -- war-audit` showed why: all 15 fell in two seeds, 28 of the 119 warnings came with too few men for the cheapest troop, and most wars came at the odds a full treasury lowers. With city projects the treasury is spent, and that is the whole of the change: a Rome that builds first levels only, so its gold sits at its limit, meets 22 wars on seeds 1 to 20 when it ignores its warnings, against 6 for one that builds every project. At 4 % with city projects, a Rome that builds and answers its warnings as the game advises, with the troop and the guards they name, loses no city over seeds 1 to 40 when it builds first levels only, against 48 when it ignores them, and 9 when it builds as the panel suggests, all in one seed's ninth year, against 2 when it ignores them: with its gold spent, wars on it are few either way. A Rome that builds nothing is lost either way: answering, it loses 133 and 131 cities on the two seed sets and falls 4 and 13 times; ignoring, 193 and 237, and falls 9 and 4 times. The owner decided to keep the rule of war as it is and to teach the player instead: the warning says what a full treasury costs, and the news says what an answer bought. Population groups by class and culture are out of scope until we ask for them.
- **Evacuation.** You order part of a city's population to leave. The people form a refugee column, which is a slow marker that walks to a city you choose. Enemy armies can catch the column. People who left cannot be sacked or enslaved.
- **Economy.** A country runs on gold, food, and manpower. Provinces hold trade goods such as iron, horses, timber, grain, and luxuries. Some troops and buildings need a specific good. Each city builds from a short list of buildings. Armies cost gold and food as upkeep, and the people of every city eat food. A city in unrest yields less gold and manpower, but its people still grow their own food, so a conquest never starves the realm that made it. Granaries hold a few months of food, a treasury holds eight months of what the people pay, and the manpower a realm can call up is a small share of its people less the men already under arms, so nothing piles up without end. The land feeds fewer men than a realm can call up, so a player who raises more men than the land feeds runs short of food and loses men until the army fits the land. There are no supply lines yet.
- **Buildings.** Agreed after the third playtest, when gold piled up unspent and long stretches passed with nothing to decide. Each city builds one building at a time from a list of five, each changing a different part of the country, so gold is a choice between an army now and a stronger country later. A forum raises a large city's gold by half. Canals grow five more food a day in any city, which feeds more men. Barracks give a city's manpower and its share of the levy half again, and train its recruits in half the days. Towers raise the full walls by half and the full garrison by three tenths. A temple counts unrest down three days a day in its owner's cities within 400 km. The gold is paid at the order, and cancelling gives back half. A city in unrest or with an enemy at its walls starts nothing, and its work waits. A city that falls keeps its finished buildings for the conqueror and loses the one at work. Occupying and enslaving keep the buildings, and sacking destroys them all. Each building pays back its cost in two to six years in the city it suits, checked with `npm run sim:buildings`. After the thirteenth play-through, in which a ten-year Rome passed years 2 to 4 and year 8 at speed 3 with nothing to decide and gold at its limit, each building has a second level and each city may hold one great work. A peaceful Rome that buys what pays back spends nearly all its gold, so the full treasury came from a player who saw nothing worth buying: the city panel gives every option's payback, and once a game, when gold first nears its limit while a city builds nothing and could start a building that pays back within the AI's years, the news names the city, the building, and its payback. A second level needs its first and pays back in about five years where it suits: Law courts add a quarter of the city's base gold to the Forum's half (7,500 gold, 75 days), Reservoirs grow five more food a day (6,500, 60), a Drill field raises the Barracks' manpower and levy from 1.5 to 1.75 times (7,500, 60), Outer walls add a quarter of the full walls and a fifth of the full garrison to the towers' (7,500, 75), and a Sanctuary carries the temple to 700 km (1,750, 45). A great work takes a year of the city's one building slot and is priced under the treasury of the realm it suits: a Treasury hall adds two fifths of the city's base gold (12,000), a Merchants' quarter raises its end of each route by two fifths and opens one more route slot (11,000), and a Stronghold, on towers, adds the full walls again and a third more garrison (12,000). Each pays back in four to five years where it suits and in more than six, or never, where it does not. The rules of buildings hold for them: one at a time, gold at the order, half back on cancelling, nothing started in unrest or under siege, a conqueror keeps what stands and loses the work, and a sack destroys them all. The AI builds them by its rule, a first level before any of them. A city also hires guards for gold, agreed after the sixth playtest, when gold sat at its limit with nothing to buy from year four and a daily upkeep held the gold still once a few bands were hired: each band of 500 serves a year for 2,000 gold paid at the hire, with no manpower, food, or upkeep, and raises the garrison at once, up to the rank's full garrison in guards, so a capital holds 3,000 more. After the seventh playtest, when re-hiring the same bands every year kept every great power from declaring for ten years, a great power counts a band against a war only for its first 90 days, while the band holds the walls for its whole year: guards bought when a warning or a war comes buy time and hold the city, and guards kept up year after year buy no lasting peace. A band leaves on its day and the news says so, so the choice comes back each year and the gold keeps moving. Hired men do not serve a conqueror. A city that holds its full garrison in guards takes a new band in the place of its oldest band that great powers no longer count, at the full price, and the news tells of the band sent home: agreed after the thirteenth play-through, when the guards bought for one warning filled the walls of Ariminum and the next warning could not be answered with 9,000 gold in hand.
- **Trade.** Trade runs along routes between cities. Caravans and ships move on the map and earn gold for both ends when they arrive. Each province keeps one seeded good, grain, iron, horses, timber, or luxuries. A homeland starts with at most three kinds, so trade reaches beyond it. Goods are not yet requirements for troops or buildings.
- **Opening a route.** A city has one route slot, plus one each for 25,000 people, a Forum, and a port. Both ends use a slot. A route pays more for unlike goods and distance. Either end may improve a running route twice, each time for half its opening price, to raise its earnings at both ends by a quarter: a step repays in about twice the route's own payback, so it is worth buying once the slots are full. The panel gives both ends' payments, the opening cost, and payback before the order. Agreed after the fifth playtest, when nothing was left to decide once the slots were full. A domestic route costs twice as much because the realm collects both ends. Closing gives no refund. An AI and a minor consent to a profitable route at peace with a free slot; an invitation to the player waits for their acceptance, charges the inviter only on acceptance, and expires after 90 days. Every AI and minor considers one route monthly by return per gold, then income, then city id, with no chance in the choice.
- **Trade and war.** War between the partners closes the route. A route whose other end is the player's passes to a partner city's new owner, and closes only if that owner is at war with the player, so a partner taken in a war the player is not part of keeps trading. A change of owner still closes every other route: when routes between other realms passed to their conquerors, a conqueror inherited its victim's trade, and a power at peace with every stronger power grew in fewer years (112 of 129, under the 90 % target). Agreed after the fifth playtest, when 22 of the player's routes died that way in ten years. An enemy army within 20 km of a road takes its cargo and stops the route while it stays. Replacement cargo waits one crossing's days, so entering and leaving the road cannot take another shipment each day. A siege of either end stops land and sea deliveries; offshore sea raiding and war-fleet blockades remain stage four. An AI's war threshold counts the trade it would cut, up to a quarter more required strength. Its peace wish is unchanged: remembering lost routes through a war belongs with the peace rule, rather than guessing the value after those routes have closed.
- **Trade accounting.** Expected receipts count in the realm's displayed income, its eight-month treasury limit, and the administrative penalty beyond eight cities. Only arrivals pay gold. A city lists only its own endpoint's income, including on a domestic route; the cities add up to the realm. This keeps a large trading realm under the same diminishing returns as a large taxing realm.
- **Unrest.** A freshly conquered city has a period of unrest.
- **Diplomacy.** Factions are at war or at peace. They can sign a trade agreement, a non-aggression pact, an alliance, and a tribute or vassal treaty. A peace deal can transfer provinces or gold. Each AI faction holds an opinion of you that moves with your actions.
- **Peace.** Agreed after the third playtest, when Rome fell from ten cities to one with no way out of its wars. Either side of a war may offer peace as things stand, or with one side paying gold, cities, or both. A war remembers who declared it, when, the cities each side took and still holds, and the men each lost in battle, and how it stands is read from those facts and the sieges under way, all of which the player can see. An AI faction answers by one plain rule with no chance in it: its wish for peace is how badly the war goes for it, plus a point for each fortnight of war, plus more for each other war against a great power and for empty stores, less a fixed resolve, and it accepts terms whose worth to it makes up the rest. Following the peace playtest and outside review, only a durable loser gets a wish floored at zero: city/battle points must be negative, with a city still held by the enemy or battle losses above 10% of surviving fielded soldiers plus those losses. A siege alone is not a defeat. When neither side has lost a city or more than that share, white peace waits until 225 war-days, unless the side is durably losing another war against a great power by that same real-loss test. Before then a side not durably losing either war must still ask a positive price; the enemy bleeding or another war alone may make it cheaper, not free. A genuine defeat on the other front removes that minimum, but the ordinary reckoning must still agree. Sacrificing the player's own men cannot make a siege of an undamaged winner buy free early peace either. These restraints are visible factors, not hidden exceptions. It does not talk in the first two months of a war. Suggested terms say Send offer and send with one click; incoming demands show the treasury against the price, and any rise names the preceding price and changed war facts or treasury constraint. The panel shows the reckoning, reason by reason, before the player offers, and warns when a battle or siege may change it. The answer uses the next day's facts; a refusal is news with its reason and, when the forecast changed, what changed it. Offers and a paused clock survive export and load. Agreed after the fifth playtest, when closing the panel or refusing ran the game on: the clock is the player's, so a game that stops for an offer, a captured city, or a win stays paused until the player resumes it, whatever they answer and whichever panel they open. When the cheapest terms the other side would take today cost the player less than its demand, the offer says so and sends them with one click. The AI offers peace by the same rule, to the player and to other powers, when terms exist that both sides would take: the side that is winning opens no more than 500 gold (five points) above the least it would take, nor more than halfway to the most the other would give, unless the other can pay only with a city worth more, when it asks the least it would take; any other side offers the least the other would take. A player who pays the first price the page shows overpays by at most that margin. An offer to the player pauses the game. A peace starts a truce of a year, in which neither side may declare war on the other. A paid peace binds longer: each 100 gold of what the payer gave, or a city's worth in points, adds 30 days, up to two more years. Agreed after a play-through in which an idle Rome paid Carthage 1,400 gold and Carthage declared war again the day the year ran out, so a bought truce must outlast the price. The two sides' sieges and battles against each other end, and their armies on each other's land go home, by sea when no road leads there. A minor city makes peace by the same rule, but a treaty never hands over a side's last city, so a minor is still taken only by siege. Checked with `npm run sim:peace` on seeds 1 to 20 over ten years: most wars between great powers end in peace, none lasts the ten years, and a pair goes back to war no sooner than its truce allows. `npm run sim -- truce` plays an idle Rome that pays every offer it can against one that refuses every offer, and prints how long a paid peace holds and how much longer Rome lives for paying. Before/after evidence is in `docs/engineering/peace-evidence.md`.
- **Progression.** A tree of 20 to 30 advances in three branches, which are military, economy, and statecraft, all inside the classical era. Built first as nine, three in each branch, agreed after the thirteenth play-through, in which years 2 to 4 and year 8 passed at speed 3 with nothing to decide and gold at its limit. A realm studies one advance at a time, in any order, each once, and pays its gold when study starts, as for a building; giving up gives back half. They are paid in gold, not research income from cities and buildings, for now: what the play-through lacked was a use for gold, and a second income would leave the gold idle again. Research income can come when the tree grows past the first nine. The nine, agreed after measuring a Rome that builds as the panel suggests, which spends nearly all its gold on buildings that pay back in about two years, so an advance must pay back as soon to be chosen: in military, Drillmasters (generals of skill 5, 3,000 gold, 180 days), Marching rations (men eat a tenth less, 3,000, 180) and Siege engineers (walls worn half again as fast, 1,500, 120); in economy, Standard coin (a fifth more gold, 6,000, 300), Fallow fields (a fifth more food grown, 6,000, 240) and Market weights (routes pay a fifth more at your ends, 5,500, 240); in statecraft, Citizen rolls (a tenth more manpower, a day and to call up, 3,000, 300), Clemency (half the unrest in a city you take, 1,500, 120) and Long contracts (guards serve 18 months for the year's price, 3,000, 150). Standard coin, Fallow fields, Market weights and Citizen rolls add to every city, so a realm of more than 8 cities pays their listed price times its cities over 8, and a large realm buys them back no sooner than Rome; the price is fixed the day study starts. Citizen rolls gives a tenth, not a fifth, because it is more men: agreed when a fifth took a realm with all nine to about 1.23 times the men of one with none. Each changes a different part of the realm through a rule that already exists, and pays back its gold in a time `npm run sim -- advances` checks; the whole military branch is worth less than a fifth more men. The AI studies an advance only when it pays back no later than the building it would start next, with gold enough or not, so an advance never puts off a building that pays sooner: agreed after a Rome played by the AI lost 28 cities in 20 games against 12 without advances, because it bought slow advances on days its next building was out of reach. The AI so studies little. The Advances panel likewise marks no advance while a city of the player's could start a building that pays back sooner, and names that building instead. With nine advances the choice runs out: a Rome that builds nothing has nothing left worth studying by Year 5, and one that builds as the panel suggests by about Year 10; this is accepted for the first nine, and the larger tree is the remedy. A finished advance is news and does not stop the game, and a realm that has studied nothing is told once, when its treasury first nears its limit, that the Advances panel exists.
- **Victory.** You win when you hold 40% of all provinces, which is 32 of the 80, and 3 of the 5 great capitals, your own among them. The great capitals are Roma, Carthago, Pella, Alexandria, and Persepolis. The game announces the win once and lets you continue. You lose when your last city falls, and the game then stops on a defeat screen. The AI powers settle at 16 to 18 cities each, so the share asks for war on the great powers and not only on the minor cities.

### Look

A flat, top-down map in the style of an old atlas. The base is parchment-toned with soft shaded relief. Coasts and rivers are inked lines. Each faction's land carries a translucent colour wash. Cities are inked symbols, and an army is a banner at the widest zooms. Closer in, armies, battles, siege camps, transport ships, and refugee columns are drawn as small figures in the map's ink and wash, in the owner's colour, that march, fight, fall, and run as the rules play the days.

The map shows mountains, hills, forests, deserts, lakes, towns, and villages as ink drawings that gain detail as you zoom in, from whole ranges and woods on the full map to single peaks, trees, and the fields around each village up close. At a close zoom a little ambient movement, such as smoke over the cities and boats off the ports, makes the world feel inhabited.

### Out of scope until asked for

Multiplayer, religion, culture, characters and dynasties, spies, detailed population groups, and supply lines.

## Stages

Each stage ends in a build you can play.

1. **The real-time war on the map.** The whole map at coarse detail, with 60 to 80 provinces and the five powers under a basic AI. Cities have population and produce gold, food, and manpower. You recruit armies, march them, cross the sea as convoys, fight battles with stances, besiege or storm cities, choose a captured city's fate, and evacuate refugee columns. Pause, speed control, and fog of war work. This stage comes first because it tests the riskiest part of the idea, which is whether managing several fronts in real time on this map is fun.
2. **Economy.** Buildings, trade goods, and trade routes on the map. Buildings come first.
3. **Diplomacy.** The treaty set and AI opinions. Peace, with terms and a truce, is the first part.
4. **War fleets.** Sea battles, blockades, and trade protection.
5. **Progression.** The advancement tree.
6. **Faction signatures.** Unique units, buildings, and bonuses.
7. **Victory.** The dominance condition.

Later work splits the coarse provinces into finer ones.

## Technology

| Layer | Choice | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Platform | TypeScript in the browser, built with Vite | A strategy game is mostly interface, and HTML and CSS are the strongest toolkit for it. A change reloads in under a second. An agent can test the rules from the command line and drive a real browser to check a feature. |
| Map renderer | PixiJS 8 | PixiJS draws with the graphics card, so pan and zoom are a camera transform. Soft fog edges and glows are filters. |
| Interface | Svelte 5, without SvelteKit | Svelte updates single values on the page when they change, which suits state that changes every tick. A one-screen game needs no routing or server rendering. |
| Rules | Plain typed data and pure functions | See the next section. |
| Tests | Vitest for rules, Playwright for the running game | Rules are tested without a browser. Features are checked in a real browser. |
| Saves | Browser storage, with export to and import from a file | The whole game state is plain data, so a save is that data written as JSON. The browser keeps one save the player writes and one the game writes at the start of each 30-day month. |
| Hosting | None yet | The build output is a static folder that any static host can serve. |

### Alternatives we set aside

- **Godot, or Rust compiled to WebAssembly.** Godot centres on a visual editor that an agent cannot operate. Rust has slow compile cycles and weak toolkits for a panel-heavy interface. Neither speed advantage is needed at a scale of a few thousand objects.
- **Plain Canvas 2D, Phaser, MapLibre, and deck.gl.** Canvas 2D draws in software, and soft fog is awkward in it. Phaser brings a game loop that competes with the simulation clock. MapLibre and deck.gl are built for web maps with a tiled Mercator camera.
- **React and SolidJS.** React re-renders by component, which needs extra discipline with per-tick state. SolidJS has the smallest ecosystem of the three.
- **A Go server with a browser client.** A server makes sense for multiplayer or for a persistent world. For a pausable single-player game, a server means that every session needs a running process, a network protocol between the two halves, and two languages. The game stays browser-only. The rules sit behind a boundary that a server could host later.

## Architecture

### The rules are a pure module

The game state is one tree of plain typed records. The tree has one collection for each kind of thing: factions, provinces, cities, armies, formations, convoys, refugee columns, battles, and sieges. Each record has a typed id, and records refer to each other by id.

The rules advance the state one game day at a time. One tick takes the current state and the orders issued since the last tick, and produces the next state. Inside a tick, the orders apply first, and then the systems run in a fixed order: movement, battles, sieges, economy, and population.

The AI is not a system. The AI is a pure function from the state to a list of orders, and the clock adds those orders to the next tick.

Four rules keep the module testable and movable:

- The rules never import the renderer or the interface.
- The player and the AI change the state only through the same list of orders. The AI cannot reach into the state.
- All randomness comes from a seeded generator that is part of the state. The same state and the same orders always give the same result.
- The state holds no functions, classes, or references to the page, so the state can be saved as JSON and loaded back.

### The clock drives the rules

A fixed-step loop on the main thread runs the ticks. A speed setting changes how many game days pass in one real second. Pause stops the ticks. Orders issued while paused wait in the list and apply on the next tick. The renderer draws between ticks and interpolates marker positions so that movement looks smooth.

### The renderer and the interface only read

The PixiJS renderer reads the state and draws it. The renderer sits behind a small boundary so that it can be replaced without a change to the rules.

The Svelte panels do not hold the live state. The live state stays plain data outside Svelte's reactivity. The panels receive read-only views of the state at a fixed refresh rate of about ten times a second. A click in a panel or on the map becomes an order.

### A build script makes the map

A Node script builds the map data file from a curated list of cities and from free geographic data. The script projects the coordinates with a regional projection, seeds one province for each city, clips the provinces to the land, and writes the province shapes, the neighbours of each province, and the city positions. It also writes terrain areas (mountains, hills, desert, forest, and lake), named peaks, and towns and villages. The game loads the output file. Nobody draws provinces by hand, and a change to the city list rebuilds the map.

Free movement uses a navigation mesh: a constrained Delaunay triangulation of the land and the sea, built from the map when the game starts, on which armies, convoys, and refugee columns find their paths. Terrain does not change movement speed yet.

### Data sources

| Source | Use | Licence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural Earth | Land, coastlines, rivers, lakes, desert outlines, and the names of ranges and peaks | Public domain |
| ETOPO1 (NOAA) | The outlines of mountains and hills | Public domain |
| RESOLVE Ecoregions 2017 | Forest outlines | CC BY 4.0, attribution required |
| Pleiades | The names and locations of ancient cities, towns, and villages | CC BY 3.0, attribution required |
| Itiner-e | Roman roads. Not used yet, because the build cannot reach its hosts | CC BY 4.0, attribution required |

The game does not use AWMC geodata (ODbL) or historical-basemaps (GPL-3.0), because both carry share-alike terms. Modern coastlines and rivers differ from the ancient ones in places such as river deltas. The first builds accept that difference.

## How we build it

One coordinating agent plans the work and checks the results. Nexus threads do the work in parallel, one pull request per thread. The coordinator writes each brief, orders the queue, reads each diff, and plays each stage build. Each brief names the evidence that proves the work is done, such as a test command and its expected output.

Parallel threads need a shared base, so the work starts in sequence.

**Foundation, in order:**

1. The project skeleton: Vite, Svelte 5, TypeScript in strict mode, Vitest, Playwright, and a check that runs on every pull request.
2. The state types, the order list, the tick loop, the seeded generator, and save and load.
3. The map build script and the map data file.

**One trial unit.** One lane goes from brief to merged pull request before the others start. A flaw in the brief format then costs one thread.

**Lanes for stage one, in parallel after the foundation:**

| Lane | Contents | Starts after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renderer | Map, colour washes, markers, camera, fog | Foundation 2 and 3 |
| Movement | Pathfinding on land, convoys at sea | Foundation 2 and 3 |
| Battles | Formations, stances, morale, retreat | Foundation 2 |
| Cities | Population, production, recruitment, upkeep, unrest, refugee columns | Foundation 2 |
| Sieges | Siege, storm, relief, and the fate of a captured city | Battles |
| Panels | Time controls, city panel, army panel, alerts | Foundation 2 and Renderer |
| AI | Faction AI that plays through the order list | Movement, Battles, Cities, Sieges |

Each rules lane owns one system file and its tests. The state has one file for each kind of record. A lane that needs a new field adds the field to that kind's file in its own pull request.

After the lanes merge, the coordinator plays the stage-one build and tunes the numbers against the tuning target.
